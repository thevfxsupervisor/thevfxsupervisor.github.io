## The job

First-round bids came in over budget, and the edit kept moving until picture lock. A Nordic feature with several hundred VFX shots. I owned the money side: bidding, vendor contracts, budget control and the client's financial reporting, from the first bid package through final delivery invoicing. The forecast final cost finished within a fraction of a percent of the agreed budget.

## What I did

Every vendor price flowed into one live forecast, so a bid moved the number the moment it landed, long before any invoice.

**Bidding.** All five vendors bid on one standard template, linked live to the master budget. Each vendor saw only its own sheet: no rates, no rival's pricing. Each received a watermarked per-shot reference set, and every new cut arrived with their own previous prices already filled in, so they only priced what changed and re-bids came back fast. Lesson worth stealing: over-locked bid sheets push vendors into unofficial copies, so protect the formulas and nothing else.

**Vendors.** The forecast counted only signed money, with the maybes tracked beside it. Vendor discounts went in once they were in the contract. Potential rebates were shown next to the forecast, never counted in it: money not yet received does not belong in the reported number. When one vendor declined part of a package, those shots moved to a second vendor without moving the budget. I caught mismatched payment terms before signature, reconciled every invoice against milestones and scope, and gave every change order its own tracked line. And when part of one package was paused, I priced the pause before anyone counted on the saving: the vendor had already started every shot, so pausing VFX work does not give you the money back.

**Budget.** The budget was frozen at the agreed figure while the forecast moved live, so variance was always on screen. Unpriced shots were carried at an internal estimate from day one, so the forecast never understated cost. Contingency sat as a visible flag through the risky middle of the show and was released once delivery risk passed. When the budget came under pressure mid-show, I did not hand over a cut list. I sorted every candidate shot: must-have for story, must-have technically, worth a creative review, or could be simplified. That sort settled the question, and not one shot was cut. The job was never to hit the first number. It was to make every move in the number visible, explained and on the record.

**Reporting.** The client got a financial pack in their own format: overview, cash flow, exposure, payments, contracts, finaling and delivery, mapped to their chart of accounts and tied to the forecast to the cent. A live working report fed a frozen client copy at a stable link and a dated PDF archive, refreshed every one to two weeks. The client flagged key shots directly in the shot-level budget report, and those flags survived every rebuild.

## How the office ran

Two specialist AI agents did the mechanical work, and I kept every judgment call and every send. One owned shot identity and delivery, the other money and reporting. They coordinated through [link-session](/projects/link-session/) and drafted the procedural emails for me to review. I handled every negotiation, answered every producer question and wrote the important emails myself.

Shot identity came from [Breakdown Studio](/projects/breakdown-studio/): shots were keyed on their on-screen slate burn-in, not timecode, so identity survived every re-cut. OCR proposed, a human confirmed, and OCR never wrote a number into the budget.

The discipline is what made the agents safe on a client's money. Every write was read back and matched by shot identity. Every audit was tested to prove it could fail. By delivery, fifteen automatic audits stood between the working file and the client, each one added after a real error. Independent audit agents, never handed the correct totals, worked each report tab and caught sign errors, double counts and stale labels before the client saw the detail. Checking every tab's totals against the others caught a flipped sign that would have shown savings as overruns.

## Security: the footage pipeline ran on our own machines

Shot detection, slate reading, thumbnails, reference clips and the cut-to-cut comparison all ran on our own machines, and vendors bid from watermarked reference clips. The only cloud model in the office was Claude Code, on a private account with training turned off. It looked at stills, mostly when we reviewed doubtful cuts together, and no other cloud AI service touched the show. And I made every send myself. On a show under NDA, that is the difference between an AI office a client can sign off and one they cannot.

## When it went wrong

Errors still happened. The agents logged their own, and every kind of error became an audit or a rule. When an unexplained jump appeared in the forecast, revision history on the live sheets traced it to a single changed line, and the contracted price was restored.

## Why this matters on your show

Overruns happen when nobody sees the cost change in time. A shot gets added, a bid changes, a scope grows, and if the report only catches it when the invoice arrives, the chance to choose has gone. I run bidding, vendors and the forecast so every change is in the client's very next report, with a production office that checks its own work first. On your show, that office runs from the first breakdown, so the bid, the forecast and the client's report are one system from day one.
